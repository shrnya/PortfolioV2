SmartSense is Ecolibrium’s energy management platform. Customers across several industries use it to keep an eye on how much energy they use, where it goes and what it costs.

SmartSense 8.0 was our chance to rethink two things: how dashboards get set up for each customer, and how people make sense of the energy flowing through their facility.

<Say seed={31}>Two halves to this one. The dashboards were very much a team effort. The SLD was my baby.</Say>

<h2><span class="no">Part A · Dashboards</span>Every customer, a dashboard from scratch</h2>

SmartSense 3.0 had grown one feature at a time, as new customers asked for new things. Years of that left legacy code, patchwork development and a product that felt bulky and hard to extend.

The dashboards were the clearest sign. Each customer got their own, with widgets and scorecards set up by the onboarding team to match how that customer worked. Lovely for the customer. Not so lovely when every new customer meant setting it all up again from scratch.

<Note>Great for the customer. Less great for the onboarding team’s calendar.</Note>

The customers weren’t all alike either. One industry might care most about Specific Energy Consumption (SEC). Another lives and breathes Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE). Same energy questions underneath, different numbers on top.

So the question became: **how do you build one structure that fits all of them, without making anyone feel squeezed in?**

<h2><span class="no">Part A · 2</span>Standardise what’s common, customise what’s different</h2>

We looked across existing customers for the patterns they shared, listened to their feedback and day-to-day needs, and turned those into reusable dashboard templates.

The layout of each scorecard, where it sits and how its trend is drawn stay the same. The KPIs inside change with the industry. Switch between three (anonymised) industries below.

<Scorecard />

<Note>Same frame, different picture. My favourite kind of system.</Note>

We didn’t want to swap one-off setups for rigid sameness either. So each template came with:

- KPIs and information that fit the customer’s industry.
- One custom dashboard per customer, where people can add, remove and rearrange widgets themselves.
- Extra widgets for specific needs, only where there was a clear case for one.

One client, for example, needed to compare energy from the grid with energy from diesel generators, and see when both ran at once. Instead of a one-off dashboard, we gave them one dedicated widget inside the standard structure. It’s the extra card under Industry C above.

<h2><span class="no">Part A · 3</span>Same data, three very different questions</h2>

Someone keeping one site running and someone overseeing sites across several countries don’t open a dashboard for the same reason. So we built the templates around three levels of responsibility, each with its own balance of “act on this now” and “think about this later”.

<RoleSwitch />

<Note>These splits were our design intent, a way to decide what goes first. Not something we measured.</Note>

- **Site level:** keeping one site healthy every day. Issues and actions come first, strategy second.
- **Region level:** looking after all the sites in one country, balancing daily oversight with finding improvements.
- **Portfolio level:** overseeing every region. Mostly big picture: cost leakage, comparisons, savings and where attention is needed. There’s still an action path, so spotting a problem can lead straight to doing something about it.

The structure follows the organisation itself: **Portfolio → Region → Site → Asset.** You can go down from wherever you sit. Someone at portfolio level can drill all the way to a single asset. Someone at site level sees their own site and everything in it.

<Say seed={33} flip>One hierarchy for both navigation and access. Easier to learn, easier to explain.</Say>

<h2><span class="no">Part B · The Single Line Diagram</span>A diagram that showed everything and said nothing</h2>

A Single Line Diagram (SLD) maps how electricity moves through a facility, from sources like the grid, solar or generators, through transformers and distribution boards, down to every machine.

The SLD in 3.0 was meant to give people one consolidated view of their energy network. But it didn’t really tell you the state of things, tracing an issue through connected parts wasn’t intuitive, and it left a lot of the platform’s data on the table.

<Note>Like a map with every street on it, but no “you are here”.</Note>

For 8.0, I wanted the SLD to become a place to diagnose problems: find the unhealthy node, follow the trail and get to the cause.

<h2><span class="no">Part B · 2</span>Two drawings of the same network</h2>

Not everyone who opens an SLD reads electrical diagrams for fun. So I designed two views of the same network.

<TwoViews />

The **simplified flowchart** is the main view. Clear connections, animated power flow and colour-coded states let you read the network without an electrical background. The **technical diagram** is there for the engineers who prefer conventional notation. It’s static, but every component is still selectable and the heat map works on it too.

Then I put the important stuff right on each node, so you don’t have to click to know what’s going on.

<NodeAnatomy />

<Note>The orange does a lot of heavy lifting here. Use it sparingly and it means “look here”.</Note>

Nodes with alerts or insights turn orange. You can see load right now, or switch to energy over the past 24 hours, 7 days or 30 days. Sources and end assets look different, so you can tell them apart at a glance. A heat map shows healthy and unhealthy areas across the network, and filters narrow it down by health, asset, online status and more.

<h2><span class="no">Part B · 3</span>Don’t fix the symptom, follow the power</h2>

Spotting a problem is half the job. The SLD needed to help people find where it actually starts.

Select a node and a side panel opens with its KPIs, the nodes upstream and downstream of it, its alerts and insights, and links to deeper asset details.

Say an air handling unit is getting a lot less power than it should. The tempting fix is the unit itself. But trace it upstream, compare what each node passes along, and the biggest loss might be sitting two steps above it. Try it:

<h2><span class="no">Part B · 4</span>From the diagram to the machine</h2>

From the side panel you can open an asset’s detail page. If the asset has an HMI or SCADA view, you can go into its processes and parameters without losing your place in the investigation.

For those assets, there’s also a simulator. Change a parameter and see how it affects the others, before touching anything for real.

<Simulator />

<Note>Only works where the customer has the infrastructure for it. Not every asset has a SCADA view, so not every node gets a simulator.</Note>

<h2><span class="no">What changed</span>From one-off and static to scalable and actionable</h2>

SmartSense 8.0 launched in September 2026, and fixes and integrations have kept coming since.

Dashboards now start from reusable, role-based templates, with a little room for each customer to make them their own, so setting up a new customer relies less on configuring every dashboard by hand. The SLD connects the whole network to node-level diagnosis, asset details and simulation where it’s supported.

The user interviews we ran after the designs were done backed up the hierarchy: what users said mattered most was what the dashboards put first.

### What I’m taking with me

- **Scalability is a design problem too.** If every new customer needs a setup of their own, the product can’t grow. Designing for the common patterns reduces that work without taking flexibility away.
- **The best dashboard isn’t the fullest one.** It’s the one that shows a particular person what matters and what to do next.
- **Data is only useful once you can act on it.** Capturing it is the easy part. Helping people read it, question it and decide is the design work.
- **Let people start broad and go deep.** Begin with the whole system, dig into the parts, and never lose your place along the way.
