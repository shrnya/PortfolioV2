<h2><span class="no">1 · The setting</span>A map from fifteen years ago</h2>

<Note>Imagine finding your way around a city with a hand-drawn map. It works, but not really.</Note>

A single-line diagram (SLD) shows how electricity moves through a building: from the grid, solar or generators, through transformers and distribution boards, to every machine that uses it.

Most energy managers had one as a printout or a PDF. No live data, no alerts, and no way to trace a fault back to where it started. To understand how power was flowing, people jumped between spreadsheets, SCADA screens and conversations.

<h2><span class="no">2 · The stakes</span>An alert with no address</h2>

When an alert fired on an air handling unit, nobody could tell at a glance which feeder it sat on, or whether something upstream was failing too. Finding out meant tracing the path by hand, while the problem was still happening.

Two kinds of people needed the answer. Energy managers wanted to see what needed attention without reading electrical notation. Engineers needed the real electrical detail before sending someone out.

<h2><span class="no">3 · What I decided</span>Three decisions</h2>

<Decision n={1} of={3} title="Let the diagram show what’s wrong">
  <Fragment slot="call">Problems appear on the diagram itself, so nobody has to sift through alert lists to find them.</Fragment>
  <p>Connectors animate in the direction power flows and are coloured by state: green for active, orange for degraded, grey for disconnected. Any node with open alerts, insights or stale data turns orange. A switch moves between live kW and historical kWh.</p>
</Decision>

<Decision n={2} of={3} title="Isolate the path in one click">
  <Fragment slot="call">Choose any node and everything off its path fades away, leaving exactly where its power comes from and the route it takes.</Fragment>
  <p>Try it on a small made-up facility. AHU-3 is already selected because it has open alerts.</p>
  <PathIsolate />
  <p>In the product, a sidebar opens beside the diagram with the node’s status, live KPIs, alerts and connected nodes. People can move through the network without losing their place.</p>
  <Clip src="/media/sld/isolate.mp4" caption="Isolating a path in the SLD." />
</Decision>

<Decision n={3} of={3} title="One network, two drawings">
  <Fragment slot="call">A simple flowchart for people who run the building, and a technical electrical diagram for people who maintain it, kept in sync.</Fragment>
  <p>Selecting a node in one view keeps it selected in the other. The technical view is a traditional SLD made clickable, so engineers get the notation they trust without giving up the live data.</p>
  <Clip src="/media/sld/technical.mp4" caption="Switching between the simple and technical views." />
</Decision>

<h2><span class="no">4 · Going deeper</span>From the diagram to the machine</h2>

Filters narrow the network by health, node type or path status, and a heatmap shows where energy use is concentrated for each type of asset.

<Clip src="/media/sld/filters.mp4" caption="Smart filters and the heatmap overlay." />

For assets with a SCADA diagram, people can jump straight to it. A simulator lets them try parameter changes and preview the effect on cost, energy and carbon before changing anything for real.

<Clip src="/media/sld/scada.mp4" caption="From an SLD node to its SCADA diagram and the simulator." />

<h2><span class="no">5 · What it changes</span>From tracing by hand to one click</h2>

The SLD replaces a static drawing with a live view of the whole system. A fault path that used to take manual tracing can be isolated in a click, and problems surface on the diagram instead of in a list.
