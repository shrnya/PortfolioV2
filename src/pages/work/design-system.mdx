<h2><span class="no">1 · The setting</span>Nine-pixel text that still got the job done</h2>

<Note>Ugly but working is a hard thing to redesign. People have learned where everything is.</Note>

Ecolibrium’s SmartSense platform helps companies monitor energy, assets and alerts across their buildings. The version customers used every day had grown fast. Screens, Figma files and components had drifted apart, and some text was as small as 9 pixels.

It still worked. Operations teams acted on alerts and got their jobs done. That made it risky to change.

<h2><span class="no">2 · The stakes</span>Change the look without breaking the habit</h2>

We had two jobs at once. The live product needed quick, safe improvements. The next version needed a proper system that every module could be built from.

Both had to respect the same people: facility teams who knew the old screens by heart, and senior stakeholders who would judge the new look in a single demo.

<h2><span class="no">4 · What shipped</span>Showing it to the people who sell it</h2>

With my manager, I put together a presentation of the design system, ran it past two colleagues in a dry run, reworked it from their feedback, and presented it to the extended team. It was well received across the company, including Sales and Application Engineering.

The light theme carried into SmartSense 8.0, and its components and tokens were set up in Storybook. Later, when we used Claude Code to rebuild the product for a large telecom customer, those tokens and components kept generated screens on-system. They did not replace judgment: we still fixed spacing, hierarchy and the order information appears in. That experience went live at the end of September 2026.

<h2><span class="no">5 · What I took with me</span>Rules make it consistent. Judgment makes it good.</h2>

From one manager I learned to make design decisions as rules that hold across a whole system. From another I learned visual hierarchy: what to make stand out, and how colour guides the eye.

A design system needs both. Tokens made consistency cheap. Deciding what a screen should say first was still a designer’s job.
